# Satya Nadella: stories

Stories he tells, kept close to his words. Each ends with when to use it in a hospital or research-center decision.

Note: the story of his son and empathy does not appear in the source notes. It is left out rather than invented. His empathy material below comes from S009.

## 1. The refounding: a non-founder resets the culture (2014, told 2022 to 2025)

When he became CEO, he felt the company had lost its mission. The PC goal had been met. He asks what was asked then: "Is it mission accomplished? Is it time to return all the money to the shareholders?" [S010]

"Being essentially the first non founder CEO, I felt the real need to, in some sense, refound the company" [S009]. He went back to one question: "The sense of purpose and mission, like why do we exist?" [S009]. He says founders run on implicit culture, but "You can get away with a lot, whereas a mere model CEO like me can’t." [S011] (so a non-founder must make culture explicit, inferred from S011).

His first letter opened: "It is a humbling experience to write this letter to you as only the third CEO in Microsoft's history." [S002]. The 2014 memo named the hard changes in short lines: "Organizations will change. Mergers and acquisitions will occur. Job responsibilities will evolve. New partnerships will be formed. Tired traditions will be questioned. Our priorities will be adjusted." [S001]. He gave a month for dialogue first: "We'll use the month of July to have a dialogue about this bold ambition and our core focus." [S001].

Use it when: a new director takes over a hospital or research center that has drifted. Name the purpose first, announce the hard changes plainly, and allow a set period of dialogue before the restructuring details.

## 2. Know-it-all to learn-it-all

He took the idea from Carol Dweck. "I must say I was lucky enough to pick the meme of growth mindset from Carol Dweck’s work and it’s done wonders." [S011]. "it's really helped us go from this, know it all to learn it alls" [S009]

Why it spread: "it is not considered new dogma from a new CEO. It is something that I think spoke to people as humans." [S010]. He let leaders make it their own: "You have to basically let even leaders bring their own personal passion to it, personal stories to it, give it some room to breathe." [S011].

The warning: people use it as a weapon. "There are times when I’m in meetings where people will look around the room and say" [S011] (who lacks a growth mindset, instead of naming their own fixed mindset, inferred from S011).

Use it when: launching a values or quality-culture program across clinical and research staff. Do not grade departments red, yellow, green. Let unit leaders tell their own stories, and watch for staff using the language to blame others.

## 3. The flame mail he did not send

Someone in a staff meeting raised an email that showed something was wrong. His first instinct: "I want to send back a flame mail" [S009]. Then he checked himself: "all that will do is cause that person to be more stressed. But I got to really look at the systemic issues here and then help them recognize to fix it." [S009]. He admits the slip: "The first time they admit a mistake, which by the way, I do sometimes. And then I have to check myself right." [S009]

The lesson he draws: "Feeling secure leads you to be more vulnerable." [S009].

Use it when: an incident report, safety event, or audit finding reaches the executive team. Look for the system failure before blaming the person who reported it, or reporting will stop.

## 4. The weekend email and the stressed middle

Senior leaders sending weekend email: "That's the shortest way to destroy a whole bunch of people's weekend" [S009]. Microsoft added a nudge feature to curb it (inferred from S009).

He spent most of his career in the middle: "for most of my 30 years of professional career, 32 years now, was in the middle" [S009]. In remote onboarding, "The biggest biggest thing that we found is that direct connection to your immediate manager" [S009]. A senior leader set up a Teams call to introduce a new hire to each colleague, then left so they could talk one to one (inferred from S009).

On empathy: "figuring out what is the lived experience of the people in your team" [S009]. "everybody is going through some tail event" [S009].

Use it when: rolling out a new shift pattern, rota, or onboarding process. Fund the head nurses and unit managers who must carry it, and check what senior behavior signals after hours.

## 5. His father's diary: a learning system

"There's a thing that I look at as my learning system. If there is one, I picked it up from my dad." [S009]. The diary logged three things: "tasks, done. People met. Ideas generated to act on." [S009].

Use it when: designing development for department heads or principal investigators. A simple daily log of work done, people met, and ideas to act on builds a learning habit cheaply.

## 6. Cricket: compete, don't admire, and play for the season

As a young player he watched a brilliant Australian opponent in awe until his coach shouted: "Compete, don’t admire." [S011]. A captain who took the ball away from a struggling bowler and then gave it back told him: "I needed you for a season, not for a match." [S011].

Use it when: benchmarking against a famous peer hospital, or deciding whether to keep backing a talented team member after a bad quarter. Learn from the peer without copying it, and judge people over a season.

## 7. Partnering with former rivals

Office shipped across platforms early as a signal of the new strategy (inferred from S011). "when you double down on such a strategy, you got to make sure that your software is available and your endpoints are available everywhere" [S011].

His rule for deals: "don't think of it as a press release" [S010]. "most of the big hits and most of the big revenue generators are quite frankly partnerships" [S010].

Use it when: a public hospital weighs a data-sharing or shared-service deal with a competing hospital, a university, or a private vendor. Check that each side gains real surplus, and plan what happens if one side turns greedy.

## 8. The security reset (2024)

After rapid AI growth, he put security above everything. He wrote: "Although we have made outstanding progress over the past year, we cannot take our permission to innovate" [S008]. "It is something we must earn." [S008]. The reset implies earlier lapses, though the letter does not describe them (inferred from S008).

Use it when: a hospital is tempted to push a new AI or digital tool live faster than its security and privacy review allows. Make security a named first priority with personal accountability.

## 9. Health care in the workflow: Epic, Nuance, DAX

At the 2023 Stripe chat he described the Epic work in plain clinical scenes. At shift end: "when you finish a shift and you want to hand off that process. Why don’t I have a summary?" [S010]. A bedside bot so as to "not have anything lost in the inbox for the physician" [S010]. Nuance would "transcribe their doctor-patient conversation and reduce physician burden" [S010]. By 2024 he reported: "On average, clinicians save more than five minutes per patient encounter. And 77 percent say it also improves documentation quality." [S008]

In rural South Africa, "Dr. Raymond Campbell saw an opportunity to bring mobile healthcare to communities where it's needed most" [S005]

Use it when: choosing an AI or digital project for a hospital. Start where workflow cost and burnout sit, such as handoffs, inboxes, and documentation, and measure minutes saved per encounter and clinician-rated quality.

## 10. Public sector at speed, and the Indian farmer

In COVID, "the state of California built 90 percent of its COVID-19 infrastructure with GitHub and Azure DevOps, reducing the time it takes to stand up a new website from 18 months to just three hours" [S006].

He remembers a farmer: "Nearly two years later, I can't stop thinking about the Indian farmer I met in January 2023." [S008]. "He was able to apply for complex government farm subsidies using just his voice, thanks to an app built with GPT 3.5." [S008]. "That rate of diffusion was unlike anything I had seen in my career." [S008]

Use it when: a public hospital assumes a digital service must take years to build, or that low-literacy patients cannot use it. Test a simple, voice-first service for access to benefits or appointments.

## 11. The waitress and the salesperson: adoption from below

A worker in Ise, Japan, "who went from waiting tables to teaching herself machine learning to digitally transform the restaurant where she worked" [S005]. At Microsoft, "when a salesperson does a pull request, that's kind of my thing about like, wow, that's a different org" [S010].

Use it when: deciding whether to let nurses, pharmacists, or lab staff build their own small tools. Give domain experts low-code tools with guardrails, and spot the early builders.
