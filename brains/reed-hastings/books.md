# Reed Hastings: Books

## No Rules Rules: Netflix and the Culture of Reinvention (2020, with Erin Meyer)

His book on how Netflix runs with few rules. He co-wrote it with Erin Meyer. In S003 he reads passages aloud and discusses them. Only a few short lines are quoted here. The rest is how he teaches the ideas.

**The core claim.** Talent density plus candor lets you remove controls. He frames it as a system, not a single rule. "it's a whole system around, how do you have great employee freedom, no rules and not have chaos? Because the benefit is that you get great flexibility and adaptation." [S006]

**The origin story.** The book starts with Pure Software. Each error got a new process, and the company lost its creativity (inferred from S003). He reads: "We had become increasingly effective and decreasingly creative." [S003] He also names the risk his system must answer: "I understood that as a company grows, if you don't manage it with policies or control processes, the organization is likely to descend into chaos." [S003]

**The 2001 layoff.** Netflix cut about a third of staff. The 80 who stayed did more work with more passion. This is where talent density comes from (inferred from S003).

**Candor.** The book links candor to his conflict avoidance at Pure and to a marriage counselor who showed him he was not living what he said (inferred from S003, S006). He reads: "I began encouraging everyone to say exactly what they really thought, but with positive intent" [S003]

**The explicit trade.** The book closes on freedom versus error prevention. He reads: "With Netflix, I hoped to promote flexibility, employee freedom and innovation, instead of error prevention and rule adherence." [S003] This is the line the council should weigh most. It states that the model gives up some error prevention by design (inferred from S003).

**Key ideas as he teaches them.**
- Context, not control. "Control is when you tell someone specifically to do something." [S003]
- Highly aligned, loosely coupled. "So you want them to be aligned, but not tightly coordinated." [S003]
- Jazz band vs. orchestra. He praises both and says the choice is about fit (inferred from S003).
- Leader as trunk, not king. "we look at our leadership team as the trunk supporting all of the real work, rather than being at the top of an organization, sort of like the king." [S003]
- Vacation by example, not policy. "And of course, I am a strong role model of taking lots of vacation." [S003]

**Limits he states while discussing it.** When the interviewer raised hospitals and minute-by-minute coordination (writer's account, S003), he replied: "And again, that's only appropriate for some types of businesses." [S003]

**How he described it on publication.** He presents the culture as a deliberate risk: "It's risky trusting employees as much as we do, giving them as much freedom as we do." [S010] He also says it is not for everyone: "Those who feel fear tend to check out because it's an unpleasant feeling." [S010] NPR reported a former employee's account of fear, and of people of color feeling less able to give frank feedback (writer's account, S010).

## The Netflix Culture Memo (company document)

Not a book, but the public text the book grew from. It began as a deck he walked new hires through after the 2002 IPO, then sent to every candidate, then posted online (inferred from S004). He liked that it looked unpolished: "that authenticity, really, people liked in the outside world" [S004] The current memo states the low-rule stance in one line: "This (almost) no rules rule gives employees the freedom to exercise their judgment." [S007] (company document)

## Beyond Entrepreneurship (Jim Collins)

The book he names as a lasting influence. He rereads the first 80 pages every year (inferred from S001). He says it "gave me permission to be myself and not try to be Mr. Charisma Flamboyant" [S001]

## Other ideas he borrows

- John Doerr, on budgeting time: "One of the best things I learned from John Doerr was setting a budget." [S001] This is a lesson from a person, not a book.
- The grammar of schooling, a borrowed term for how deeply school routines resist change: "an early lesson in sort of the depth and strength of the grammar of schooling that we have." [S002]
- Bloom's two sigma tutoring finding, which he is funding a study to retest (inferred from S002).
- The one-way door and two-way door idea he credits to Jeff Bezos (inferred from S001).
