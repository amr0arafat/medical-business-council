# Andy Grove: frameworks

Named, reusable models Grove uses. Each is tied to a source id. Health care, measurement, management, and public institutions come first where the notes allow.

## 1. Output is the measure of a manager

Grove judges managers by what their unit produces, not by traits, title, or presence. He saw at Fairchild what happens when nothing tangible is expected: good people, no pressure, no transfer of work to the next stage. A unit with no internal or external discipline drifts toward its own priorities.

- "Effective managers are distinguished by what they accomplish, not by what they are." [S013]
- "I guess no pressure because very little was expected in terms of tangible output." [S001]
- "There was no internal discipline to the place; and there was no external discipline or expectations that were put on the lab" [S001]
- On a service lab with no deadlines: "They took forever to do their job, picked their own priorities." [S001]

## 2. Find the binding constraint (bottleneck thinking)

Grove looks for the step that actually limits throughput, which is often not the obvious one. A fast component is worth only what the slowest step lets through. He applied this to chips, to batteries, and to the national economy. For a hospital, the useful question is which step chokes the flow, not which unit looks busiest (inferred from S004, S005).

- "Processor performance is only as useful as the I/O allows it to be." [S004]
- "we recognized that performance improvement was not limited by the silicon, but rather by the single instruction path." [S005] (S005 is a summary from his speaker notes.)
- "But the critical limitation is going to be battery production." [S009]

## 3. Invention versus scaling

Innovation has two phases. The prototype is one. The second is where you work out the details, make it affordable, build the plant, and hire people. Grove says the second phase is the hard and undervalued one, and that it requires committing capital before the market is proven.

- "Scaling is hard work but necessary to make innovation matter." [S008]
- "The decision to build these plants needed to be made years before we knew whether the Pentium chip would work or whether the market would be interested in it." [S008]
- On hesitation in memory chips: "Our Japanese competitors didn’t hesitate: They built the plants." [S008]
- Losing the scaling phase has a second cost: "we broke the chain of experience that is so important in technological evolution." [S008]

## 4. Build a crude ratio that tells the truth (output per input)

When the official numbers hide the real result, Grove invents a simple ratio and does the arithmetic in public. For jobs he divided money invested by employees ten years later. Intel came to about $650 per job and Silicon Valley later to about $100,000 (inferred from S008). He also warns that optimizing a local metric can sink the larger system.

- "First, take the initial investment plus the investment during a company’s IPO. Then divide that by the number of employees working in that company 10 years later." [S008]
- "Can we afford all this? Let's do the math." [S006]
- "If profit margins are the problem, we go to work on margins, with exquisite focus." [S008]

## 5. Strategic Inflection Point (SIP)

A SIP is a change so deep that the strategy itself must change, usually along with the core competencies. Grove draws it as a curve that bends subtly and then sends the business up or down. Once on a branch, reversal is very hard. In 1998 he told physicians and health executives that medicine was at one, driven by consumers.

- "A Strategic Inflection Point is that which causes you to make a fundamental change in business strategy. Nothing less is sufficient." [S003]
- The SIP "requires a fundamental change in a company's business strategy and generally a change in the core competencies of the business itself." [S004]
- "Once you begin your ascent or descent, the course that you've committed to tend to be very difficult to reverse." [S004]
- "We are in a period of major change that is dictated by the fact that consumers of health care services are ahead of the profession in their embrace of electronic means of getting information" [S002]

## 6. The 10X force

Grove builds on Porter's five forces. A SIP happens when one force grows about tenfold, for example when a new way of doing the work is ten times more effective (inferred from S004).

- "For instance, what happens if the way of doing something can be done in a differently and becomes ten times more effectively?" [S004]

## 7. Warning signs: silver bullet test and strategic dissonance

He admits there is no clean rule for spotting a SIP. He uses tests. If the competitor you would shoot first changes, the landscape may have shifted. When what people do drifts from what the strategy says, the actions are usually right, because they are driven by daily competition. Senior managers notice last; the front line notices first.

- "I have this mental silver bullet test. If you had one bullet, who would you shoot with it?" [S003]
- "there is a growing divergence, a dissonance if you will, between the strategy statements of those companies and the strategy actions." [S003]
- "Almost always it hits the corporation in such a way that those of us in senior management are among the last ones to notice." [S003]

## 8. Let chaos reign, then rein in chaos

The response has stages: denial, escape or diversion, acceptance, then action. First, let units experiment and watch closely what they learn. Then pass through the painful middle (the Valley of Death), picture the new industry, and concentrate resources (inferred from S003). Act early, and put actions in place before announcing them.

- "So I describe it as letting chaos reign, or experimentation, because you are not likely to start out of the chute and restructure your corporation in the first sign of something." [S003]
- "watch them like a hawk to see what you are going to learn from them." [S003]
- "if you want to prosper on the other side of the Strategic Inflection Point, you must take action before you get there." [S002]
- "It is important to put the actions in place before you start talking about it" [S003]

## 9. The yeses must equal the nos

Every organization is finite. Each new commitment needs a matching thing you stop doing. People volunteer additions and never volunteer cuts. For a hospital, a new program should name what it replaces (inferred from S003).

- "When I say the yeses have to equals the nos, that's what I mean. And everybody is very eager to give you advice how to put yeses to the balance sheet and nobody ever volunteers how to put nos." [S003]
- "with a finite organization, which is any organization, period, you have to balance out the stuff that you want to do" [S003]

## 10. Segment Zero (good enough)

Grove's version of the Christensen effect. A cheaper, inferior approach wins the bottom of the market, then climbs. Incumbents describe that segment in dismissive terms. The test is whether it is good enough for a large and growing group. Intel's answer was a separate team with its own values and a cost focus.

- "It doesn't matter how inferior the new approach is. What matters is, is the new approach is good enough for the market?" [S003]
- "The question to ask is: Is it good enough? Is it good enough for a large segment? Is the segment for whom it is good enough growing?" [S003]
- "It is small. It is obsolete. It is bottom fishing. It is our rebar." [S003]
- "Not everything that's low cost is a Segment Zero." [S003]
- "you must price the market and cost must follow price." [S003]

## 11. Helpful Cassandras and the limits of data

Grove runs on data, but he warns that data describe the past and can be a hiding place. Early signals live outside the numbers, with people who bring bad news. Fear must be removed or those people go quiet. In Tedlow's account, Grove's culture made knowledge power beat position power, and an idea without data was only a story (journalist's account, S012).

- "Don't argue with the emotions, argue with the data." [S003]
- "the data that deal with your business are pertinent to your past, and don't say anything about your future." [S003]
- "You can always hide behind the data." [S003]
- "Unless you deal with this fear, unless you live this fear you will never hear from those helpful Cassandras and you are going to be late in responding to the Strategic Inflection Points." [S003]

## 12. The revolving door (new-CEO test)

When stuck, imagine you were fired and a new leader arrived. Ask what that person would do, then do it yourself. This is how Grove and Moore broke the deadlock on leaving memory chips in 1985 (journalist's account, S012).

- "If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?" [S012]
- "Why shouldn’t you and I walk out the door, come back, and do it ourselves?" [S012]

## 13. The growth rate at which everybody fails

From his private journals in the early 1970s. Some people are misplaced from the start, some cannot grow with their jobs. Above a certain growth rate, failure becomes general. He saw finding that rate as his top job.

- "The point is, there is a growth rate at which everybody fails and the whole situation results in a chaos." [S012]
- "to identify the maximum growth rate at which this wholesale failure phenomenon begins." [S012]

## 14. Two and only two programs (incremental reform)

For health-care reform he rejects comprehensive plans. Society resists fundamental change outside a crisis. Pick two concrete fixes, prove delivery, then go bigger. His two in 2007 were emergency-room standards with an adjacent walk-in clinic, and home monitoring for the elderly (inferred from S006).

- "I propose that you commit to two - and only two - programs." [S006]
- "Fix specific problems of the system. Leave bigtime changes for later, after you've demonstrated that you can solve more limited problems." [S006]
- "Society is simply resistant to fundamental change. Dramatic departures can take place only in a crisis." [S006]

## 15. Set the standard, fund it separately, pay only those who meet it

Grove's mechanism for public programs. Define a measurable standard. Raise a dedicated levy (a 1% surtax on health-care billings, a tax on offshored labor) and keep it in its own pool. Release money only to institutions that meet the standard (inferred from S006, S008).

- "The first step in fixing this mess is to establish minimum standards for all ERs." [S006]
- "The same goes for acceptable patient waiting times: Set the standard, and implement it." [S006]
- "Require a triage system that directs patients to either the ER or the clinic, using well-defined criteria." [S006]
- "Keep that money separate." [S008]

## 16. The generalist as integrator

Specialists favor their own tools, and medicine had no neutral gatekeeper. So the patient, or the manager, must compare results across all methods. He contrasts medicine with device research, where you were expected to compare your data with others'. He also flags selection bias in outcome data.

- "There is no good gatekeeper in this business." [S007]
- "The tenors always sang tenor, the baritones, baritone, and the basses, bass." [S007]
- "The right answer, in my view, can be arrived at only by comparing the results achieved by the practitioners of all the different treatment forms." [S007]
- "Medical practitioners primarily tended to publish their own data; they often didn’t compare their data with the data of other practitioners, even in their own field" [S007]

## 17. A better measurement speeds learning

A more sensitive measure shortens the feedback loop and resets a whole field. PSA did this for prostate cancer. He wanted outcome tables made public.

- "The most important thing I learned was that the use of the PSA test reset the entire field of prostate cancer studies." [S007]
- "But since PSA can detect recurrence much earlier, the learning process about the effectiveness of treatment is accelerated." [S007]

## 18. Personal balance sheet under uncertainty

Facing treatment choices in 1995, he listed pros and cons, reduced the choice to one key unknown, and picked the option that bought time (inferred from S007). He trusted his own plotted data over any single opinion.

- "The data said I had about an even chance. I kept riding my bike." [S007]
- "I decided to bet on my own charts." [S007]

## 19. The Sporck role (operational discipline)

Every organization needs someone who enforces discipline across boundaries: training, non-performance, transfer of work. Leaders who will not remove a non-performer let the organization decay (inferred from S001).

- "In this context, I think my role at Intel was the Charlie Sporck role. It worked." [S001]
- "The reason Fairchild disintegrated was because neither Bob nor Gordon was capable of removing a Tom Bay" [S001]
- "every trouble or any misgiving I have ever had about Gordon was a case of inaction." [S001]

## 20. Strategic recognition

Much strategy is noticing an opportunity and then exploiting it, not planning it in advance.

- "You bumble along until an opportunity comes and you realize the potential of it and you mine it." [S001]

## 21. Ten-years-back forecasting rule

- "I have a rule in my business: To see what can happen in the next ten years, look at what has happened in the last ten years." [S007]

## 22. Board as gyroscope

The board's job is to keep the institution steady beyond any one leader or cycle. Directors should be tied visibly to outcomes. He links strategy to governance.

- "So you’re looking for the board to act as a gyroscope, to stabilize the company over time." [S010]
- "For them to own a piece of the action, they need to be visibly associated with the outcome" [S010]
- "After all, good strategy is good governance." [S010]

## 23. Ethics mirror test

Picture the people whose respect you want, then imagine explaining your conduct to them.

- "Picture yourself facing a group of people who are very important to you, whose opinion you value, and whose respect you seek." [S013]
- "Now, imagine yourself having to account to these people for your conduct in these situations." [S013]

## 24. Government with a built-in exit

For strategic industries, government sets priorities under urgency, then hands off and steps away. He cites the wartime production model and licensing technology to private firms (inferred from S009).

- "Then let this organization license the manufacturing technology to private companies and let it go out of business." [S009]
