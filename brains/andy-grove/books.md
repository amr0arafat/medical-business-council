# Andy Grove: books

Books he wrote or cites, as he describes them in the notes. No book text is reproduced here.

## Books he wrote

### Only the Paranoid Survive (1996)

His book on strategic inflection points. In talks he uses it as the base for the whole SIP framework and refers listeners back to it.

- "For those who have not read my last book, I will do a quick review of what Strategic Inflection Points are about." [S003]
- "Let me start by talking a little bit about what strategic inflection points are which is the gist of my book, SAP was kind enough to give each of you a copy of." [S004]
- On the PC shift: "the manufacturers of mainframes afterward acted exactly like I describe in the book." [S003]
- Tedlow says the title entered the lexicon along with the phrase strategic inflection point, and that the 1985 memory-exit story with Moore is recounted there (journalist's account, S012).
- The title phrase was already his rule of thumb in 1995, which Fortune calls Grove's Law (journalist's account, S011). The profile quotes it as "Only the paranoid survive." [S011]

### High Output Management (1983)

The notes contain no talk or interview where Grove himself discusses this book. Tedlow says Grove distilled much of his thinking in it, and calls it still worth reading (journalist's account, S012). Its core idea, judging managers by output, appears in his own words elsewhere: "Effective managers are distinguished by what they accomplish, not by what they are." [S013]

### One-on-One with Andy Grove (1987)

A collection of answers to about 150 reader questions from his syndicated advice column (journalist's account, S013). The reviewer says the most frequent question was what makes a successful manager. Grove's answer, as quoted in the review: "I advise aspiring managers to stop worrying about what magic characteristics they need; instead I urge them to concentrate on the task at hand and use their own strengths and capabilities to the best effect" [S013]. In the interview that went with the review he added: "It's a hard truth to accept. People want to be told if I do such and such, then I will be a good manager" [S013].

### Physics and Technology of Semiconductor Devices (1967)

His first book, written from a Berkeley course he taught (inferred from S001). He describes writing it lecture by lecture and the letdown when it was done.

- "When the book came out, it was like somebody blew out the light." [S001]

### Swimming Across

The notes say nothing about this memoir. No claims are made here.

### A third book on management (1995)

In 1995 Fortune reported he was working on a third book, about managing (journalist's account, S011). The notes do not name it.

## Books and thinkers he cites

### Peter Drucker, The Practice of Management (1954)

His favorite book. He found it in the 1970s. He used its chapter on the ideal chief executive (an outside man, a man of thought, a man of action) to describe the Noyce, Moore, and Grove team, and handed copies to both partners (inferred from S001). In 1998 he told the Academy of Management that reading it was how he learned the form of management (inferred from S003).

- "It’s my favorite book." [S001]
- "We couldn’t have had a better casting for those roles." [S001]
- "That kind of proves a couple of points. One is that some of these books are actually read by practitioners, which is reassuring for those of us who write books." [S003]

### Clayton Christensen (the disruption book)

He borrowed Christensen's minimill example and turned it into Segment Zero. He credits the idea openly.

- "But I'm going to go back to Christensen's book and use an example he developed there which is the onset of the minimills and the integrated steel mills." [S003]
- "Christensen gave a seminar at Intel, and I was looking at his slide as I was talking about the industry, and I had this creeping feeling in my gut that what he was really talking about was the computer industry" [S003]

### Michael Porter (five forces)

He starts the SIP framework from Porter's competitive forces and asks what happens when one force grows tenfold (inferred from S004). No book title is named in the notes.

### Alan Greenspan (the X Factor)

Not a book. He opened his 1998 health keynote with Greenspan's phrase for the unknown effect of information technology on the economy (inferred from S002).

### Other reading

During his cancer treatment he read a patient-and-doctor book on prostate cancer, found it thorough but noncommittal, and went to the original papers (inferred from S007). "By night, I read scientific papers, plotting and cross-plotting the data from one paper with the results from another." [S007]
