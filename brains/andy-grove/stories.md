# Andy Grove: stories

Anecdotes Grove tells, one per major point. Kept close to his words and tagged. S010 to S013 are journalist pieces: only his quoted words are his.

## The Saturday exodus at Stauffer

As a young man in a summer lab job, he saw workers watch the window until the senior person's car left on Saturday. "within ten minutes after the senior guy left the whole place was empty." [S001] It shaped his dislike of cultures where presence stands in for output (inferred from S001).

Use it when: a unit measures attendance or hours instead of results.

## The Fairchild lab with no pressure

At Fairchild research the people were good, but nobody expected anything (inferred from S001). "I guess no pressure because very little was expected in terms of tangible output." [S001] Work rarely crossed into manufacturing. Moore "left it to chance that any product of ours or any knowledge of ours would ever cross organizational boundaries." [S001] He drew his lesson: "I learned a lot about management with a multiplier of minus one." [S001] "I came to Intel with a ferocious determination not to build another Fairchild." [S001]

Use it when: a research center or innovation unit has talent but no output targets and no path into operations.

## Waiting two hours for Tom Bay

During a major recall, Grove waited two hours for a division head who showed up late and drunk (inferred from S001). Nobody removed him. "The reason Fairchild disintegrated was because neither Bob nor Gordon was capable of removing a Tom Bay" [S001]

Use it when: leaders tolerate a senior non-performer because removing him is uncomfortable.

## Stopping the meeting for Gordon

In a noisy meeting he saw on Moore's face that Moore had the answer. He yelled for quiet. "But somebody had to stop the traffic." [S001] "Nobody would have had access to Gordon’s insight without my recognizing that it was time to stop the bulls." [S001]

Use it when: a committee is loud but the quiet expert, often a clinician, has not been heard.

## Line 2 does not follow from line 1

At Berkeley he asked a professor about an error on the board. The professor erased it, and the class quietly fixed their notes (inferred from S001). His lesson: "These toads don’t know anymore than I do. They just don’t dare to speak up." [S001]

Use it when: staff defer to rank instead of raising a problem they can see.

## The memory exit and the revolving door

In 1985 profits were collapsing. Grove asked Moore a question. "If we got kicked out and the board brought in a new CEO, what do you think he would do?" [S012] Moore said the new CEO would leave memories. "I stared at him, numb" [S012] Then: "Why shouldn’t you and I walk out the door, come back, and do it ourselves?" [S012] Even so, "I had a hard time getting the words out of my mouth without equivocation." [S012] He later put it plainly: "It’s not that I could see the possibilities of microprocessors going into them, but I could see the horrors of the memory business by staying in it." [S001]

Use it when: a hospital must close or exit a legacy service line that defines its identity.

## Hesitating on memory, betting on Pentium

On memory chips Intel hesitated. "Our Japanese competitors didn’t hesitate: They built the plants." [S008] Later Intel spent billions on Pentium plants before the chip existed. "The decision to build these plants needed to be made years before we knew whether the Pentium chip would work or whether the market would be interested in it." [S008] "Fortunately, they gave their OK even as they gulped. The bet paid off." [S008]

Use it when: deciding whether to build beds, ORs, or labs before demand is fully proven.

## The Pentium flaw

In 1994 a flaw in the Pentium became a public crisis. Grove treated it as a technical debate (journalist's account, S012). His apology was grudging: "We apologize. We were motivated by a belief that replacement is simply unnecessary for most people. We still feel that way." [S012] Intel took a $475 million write-down. The lesson, in Tedlow's words, is that the customer's view becomes your reality (journalist's account, S012).

Use it when: a quality or safety problem looks small to experts but large to patients and the public.

## Seduced by RISC

Grove nearly dropped Intel's main chip line for a newer design. "We … I … almost walked away from it because the elegance of a new product seduced me into taking my eye off the market." [S012] Two colleagues talked him out of it (journalist's account, S012). "We almost wrecked the company" [S012]

Use it when: leaders are drawn to an elegant new program and need staff who will push back.

## Testing his own prostate cancer care

Diagnosed in the mid-1990s, he read the research himself. "By night, I read scientific papers, plotting and cross-plotting the data from one paper with the results from another." [S007] He checked the lab: "So I also decided to test the tests." [S007] He sent blood to two labs and got 6.0 and 6.1 (inferred from S007). At a joint meeting each specialist backed his own field. "The tenors always sang tenor, the baritones, baritone, and the basses, bass." [S007] He asked his radiation doctor a direct question. "If you had what I have, what would you do?" [S012] The doctor said he would probably have surgery, because training made it the gold standard (journalist's account, S012). Grove weighed even odds, kept cycling, and chose radiation. "I decided to bet on my own charts." [S007] His advice: "Investigate things, come to your own conclusions, don’t take any one recommendation as gospel." [S007]

Use it when: a care pathway, purchase, or protocol is driven by specialty habit rather than compared outcomes.

## Patients outrunning the profession

Grove described searching CompuServe from a mountain cabin after his diagnosis. "I found basic information, review papers, reviews papers written by some pretty prominent doctors, but more importantly, I rapidly stumbled on what in today's terminology would be described as a support group" [S002] Told doctors that patients now arrive with printouts, he said: "So if nothing else, self-defense compels you to take some action." [S002]

Use it when: deciding on patient portals, online access, or digital engagement.

## The e-mail secretary

Early on, his secretary printed his e-mails, he scribbled answers, and she typed them. "And I finally came to the conclusion that this is utter nonsense and it throws out all the benefits of the immediacy of e-mail." [S002] Physicians feared floods of patient mail. He answered: "although there are 70,000 Intel employees who have access to my e-mail address, they don't send me e-mails." [S002]

Use it when: staff fear a new channel will overload them, or a go-between is added that kills a tool's benefit.

## The patched legacy system

Intel's own IT kept changing slowly. "Every change in our business environment was reflected in requests for software modifications that ended up sitting in a queue for many months, sometimes many years." [S004] "Instead, the system was patched and patched and patched, until it finally required a fundamental overhaul." [S004]

Use it when: a hospital keeps patching an old records or billing system instead of replacing it.

## Segment Zero and rebar

Steel minimills started with rebar and climbed to structural steel and plate. Intel had dismissed cheap PCs the same way (inferred from S003). Insiders always describe the low end alike: "It is small. It is obsolete. It is bottom fishing. It is our rebar." [S003] Intel responded fast: "in one quarter, we took 650 engineers and put them to work on this Segment Zero microprocessor road map." [S003]

Use it when: a low-cost competitor such as a retail clinic or ambulatory center is being dismissed.

## Learning to shut up as chairman

As chairman, Grove heard a warning from director Arthur Rock. "Their concern was my tendency to speak up too much, to suck all of the oxygen out of the room." [S010] "I can change. I can submerge myself. Give me a chance to prove it." [S010] In 1998 he stayed silent on a deal, and the board killed the new CEO's first acquisition, Fore Systems. He felt he had humiliated the CEO and rebuilt the board's education (journalist's account, S010).

Use it when: a dominant chair or founder crowds out the board, or directors vote on what they do not understand.

## The employment arithmetic

He built a crude ratio: "First, take the initial investment plus the investment during a company’s IPO. Then divide that by the number of employees working in that company 10 years later." [S008] Intel came to about $650 per job. Silicon Valley today is near $100,000 (inferred from S008).

Use it when: judging what an investment actually produces per dollar, such as cost per patient served.
