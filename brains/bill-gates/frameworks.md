# Bill Gates: frameworks

Pillar: Money, impact, and measurement. Core question: What does it cost, what measurable result does it buy, and is the funding sustainable?

These are the named models he uses again and again. Each one is stated in his terms, with verbatim quotes and source ids. Notes marked (inferred from S0xx) are summaries, not quotes.

---

## 1. The goal, approach, measure, adjust loop

This is his core model. Set a clear goal. Pick the key variable that drives it. Plan the change, measure it, and use the result to adjust. He thinks most efforts fail at the measure, not at the goal.

- "You can achieve amazing progress if you set a clear goal and find a measure that will drive progress toward that goal-in a feedback loop similar to the one Rosen describes." [S003]
- "Given a goal, you decide on what key variable you need to change to achieve it" [S003]
- "You use the measurement as feedback to make adjustments. I think a lot of efforts fail because they don't focus on the right measure or they don't invest enough in doing it accurately." [S003]
- "The process I have described-setting clear goals, picking the right approach, and then measuring results to get feedback and refine the approach continually-helps us to deliver tools and services to everybody who will benefit." [S003]

In a company, profit is the given goal and managers pick intermediate measures. A public hospital or a government has no such given goal. So choosing the goal and the measure is the main discipline (inferred from S003).

Council use: ask for the goal, the key variable, the measure, and the review cycle before money is approved.

## 2. Four stages for cutting through complexity

His earlier form of the same loop, from 2007. Name the goal. Find the highest-leverage approach. Fund the ideal tool. Meanwhile, use the best tool you already have. Never wait for the ideal tool before acting.

- "Cutting through complexity to find a solution runs through four predictable stages: determine a goal, find the highest-leverage approach, discover the ideal technology for that approach, and in the meantime, make the smartest application of the technology that you already have" [S006]
- "The highest-leverage approach is prevention. The ideal technology would be a vaccine that gives lifetime immunity with a single dose." [S006]

## 3. See the problem, see the solution, see the impact

People do care. Complexity is what blocks action. A proposal must show the problem in numbers, the specific fix, and how impact will be shown and shared.

- "To turn caring into action, we need to see a problem, see a solution, and see the impact. But complexity blocks all three steps." [S006]
- "The barrier to change is not too little caring; it is too much complexity." [S006]
- "is to measure the impact of your work and share your successes and failures so that others learn from your efforts." [S006]

## 4. Cost per unit of outcome

He turns money into outcomes: years of life, people on treatment, children saved. Then waste and underfunding show up as lost outcomes. This is the test he asks his own staff to apply to every grant.

- "With a handful of new vaccines, we should be able to save a year of a person’s life for well under $100. If we waste $500,000, we are wasting 5,000 years of life." [S001]
- "This is the kind of trade-off I ask our employees to consider when they are deciding which areas to get involved in and which grants to make." [S001]
- "That will mean every $300 that governments invest in the Global Fund will put another person on treatment for a year. Every $300 that’s not forthcoming will represent a person taken off treatment. That’s a very clear choice." [S002]
- "It comes to less than $5,000 per child saved" [S004]

He shows his method and its bias. The $5,000 figure is total donor health aid divided by child deaths averted, and he names what it leaves out (inferred from S004).

- "This calculation does not take into account how child mortality might have declined without aid, which would increase the cost per life saved." [S004]

## 5. The market-failure test: who should pay for what

Each funding source has a job. Markets serve those who can pay. Government and philanthropy serve those who cannot. A foundation belongs only where markets fail and where risk capital is needed. Recurring infrastructure belongs to government.

- "Capitalism harnesses self-interest in a helpful and sustainable way, but only on behalf of those who can pay. Government aid and philanthropy channel our caring for those who can’t pay." [S007]
- "Foundations are not needed in areas where capitalistic market signals work well and the poorest aren’t left out." [S001]
- "This is why we tend not to fund other important things like building health clinics or roads, which are better left to governments." [S001]
- "These investments are high-risk and high-reward. But the reward isn’t measured by financial gain, it’s measured by the number of lives saved or people lifted out of poverty." [S001]

Council use: grants and philanthropy for risky pilots; government budgets to scale what is proven; markets for what patients or payers can buy (inferred from S007).

## 6. Innovation first, then delivery at scale

Invention is not impact until it reaches people. He treats delivery as its own design problem, with its own innovation. Lab results are not field results.

- "Innovation is the means, and equity is the end goal." [S002]
- "This innovation to reduce the delivery bottleneck is critical." [S003]
- "We have to develop and test overall delivery systems, including communication, support, and incentives, in ways that go beyond what a medical trial alone typically does." [S002]
- "In studies where the patients used the tool as they were supposed to, the results were quite good. However, in most studies the levels of usage were low and thus the overall results were disappointing." [S002]

Council use: price the full delivery chain, including cold storage and staff, not just the new product (inferred from S001).

## 7. Feedback systems people trust: the teacher example

His clearest measurement case outside health. Use several measures: test gains, trained observers, and student surveys. The point is specific feedback, not just a score. It costs real money, and paying for it is the test of seriousness. It maps directly to staff appraisal in a hospital (inferred from S002, S003).

- "I was amazed to learn a few years ago that over 90 percent of teachers get zero feedback on how to improve." [S003]
- "The evaluations are used to give a teacher not only a score but also specific feedback on areas to improve and ways to build on their strengths." [S003]
- "Dedicating 2 percent of teachers to do this work is a large investment. It can mean raising the average class size by 2 percent or spending 2 percent more money." [S002]
- "I see the willingness to make this investment as a test of whether people are serious about an evaluation system that really works." [S002]
- "I think the most critical change we can make in U.S. K-12 education is to create teacher feedback systems like the one in Eagle County that are properly funded, high quality, and trusted by teachers." [S003]

The first Eagle County system failed with teachers. The later one survived two years of budget cuts (inferred from S003).

## 8. Targets, dashboards, and the independent check

Targets motivate, and they also invite over-reporting. So he pairs routine reporting with an impartial layer: independent staff, random checks, maps, GPS. When two data sources conflict, investigate. Do not report the favorable one.

- "Each indicator had an annual target and a quarterly target." [S003]
- "Setting targets for immunization and other interventions can motivate government health workers, but it can also encourage over-reporting to avoid problems with supervisors." [S003]
- "The work required specially trained staff working independently of the people implementing the vaccination campaigns. That impartiality was crucial." [S003]
- "Ethiopia could have ignored this conflict and reported the most favorable data. Instead, it brought in independent experts to understand why the measurements were so different." [S003]

## 9. Peer comparison as a lever

Clear goals let leaders compare units, find the best, and copy them. Being behind on a public table drives action. Under Jim Grant, a single coverage goal plus a data system lifted vaccine coverage from 17 to 75 percent (inferred from S003).

- "The countries that were falling behind were embarrassed, and they focused more resources and attention on the problem than they would have without the data." [S003]
- "The UN agencies, donor countries, and developing countries looked at which programs would achieve the goals at the lowest cost." [S003]
- "if other companies in a sector simply matched what the leader in that sector is doing, we would make a dramatic impact against the world’s inequities." [S007]

## 10. Creative capitalism and market-shaping tools

Stretch market forces so more people can profit, or gain recognition, by serving those who cannot pay. Where profit is impossible, recognition stands in for it. Policy tools create markets: priority review vouchers, advance market commitments, tiered pricing, and pricing backward from what buyers can pay.

- "an approach where governments, businesses, and nonprofits work together to stretch the reach of market forces so that more people can make a profit, or gain recognition, doing work that eases the world’s inequities." [S007]
- "In markets where profits are not possible, recognition is a proxy; where profits are possible, recognition is an added incentive." [S007]
- "I believe the highest-leverage work that governments can do is to set policy to create market incentives for business activity that improves the lives of the poor." [S007]
- "it had to be priced at under 50 cents a dose" [S007]

## 11. Catalytic versus sustained funding

Philanthropy takes the first risk and builds the channel. Then governments and the countries themselves must carry the recurring cost. Pooled buying drives unit prices down. Finishing a program has its own payback. Keep budgets flexible so the plan can change.

- "Instead of trying to introduce a new product and appeal to customers, we were trying to prove to the world that an international partnership for vaccines was not only possible but necessary." [S005]
- "Once the Global Fund was established, we knew the world had a pipeline to get new innovations out to the places that needed them the most." [S005]
- "a single dose of the pentavalent vaccine, which protects against five deadly infections, used to cost $3.65. It now costs less than a dollar." [S005]
- "Many low- and middle-income countries will develop enough to pay for this convergence themselves. Others will need continued generosity from donors, including investments in health-related R&D." [S004]
- "Once we get rid of polio, the world will save about $2 billion a year that it now spends fighting the disease." [S004]
- "Sadly, some of the gains didn't last. Once the vaccination goal was reached, donor attention shifted elsewhere and coverage rates drifted down in many countries." [S003]

## 12. Aid quality questions and waste in proportion

Stop asking whether aid works; ask how it can work better. Three tests: is the recipient in charge, is local expertise being built, are the best performers sharing lessons. Fight leakage, but treat it as a tax, not a reason to stop.

- "Above all, I hope we can stop discussing whether aid works, and spend more time talking about how it can work better." [S004]
- "Are donors helping local teams build up the expertise they need to put the Western experts out of business?" [S004]
- "Suppose small-scale corruption amounts to a 2 percent tax on the cost of saving a life. We should try to reduce that. But if we can’t, should we stop trying to save lives?" [S004]

## 13. Preparedness as a standing system

Like an army, preparedness must exist before the crisis. His pieces: strong primary health systems, a medical reserve corps, links to the military for logistics, simulations, and R&D on vaccines and diagnostics (inferred from S008). The cost is modest next to the harm.

- "the problem wasn't that there was a system that didn't work well enough, the problem was that we didn't have a system at all." [S008]
- "We need to do simulations, germ games, not war games, so that we see where the holes are." [S008]
- "Now I don't have an exact budget for what this would cost, but I'm quite sure it's very modest compared to the potential harm." [S008]

## 14. AI in health and productivity, with its risks

Three principles: balance fears with benefits; markets will not serve the poorest unaided; we are only at the start (inferred from S009). Near term, AI frees health workers from paperwork. Health AI must be tested and regulated, but no care at all is also a harm.

- "filing insurance claims, dealing with paperwork, and drafting notes from a doctor’s visit." [S009]
- "AIs will help the health workers they do see be more productive." [S009]
- "AIs have to be tested very carefully and properly regulated, which means it will take longer for them to be adopted than in other areas. But then again, humans make mistakes too. And having no access to medical care is also a problem." [S009]
- "market forces won’t naturally produce AI products and services that help the poorest. The opposite is more likely." [S009]
- "AIs also make factual mistakes and experience hallucinations." [S009]

## 15. Limits and mistakes he admits

He names his misses and moves to the fix. Small schools did not lift achievement. Common Core adoption was not enough, and the market did not respond. Education programs lost their effect at scale. Daily HIV prevention pills worked in trials but not in delivery. He also expected to over-apply business habits.

- "I am sure I will make mistakes in over-applying some elements from my previous experience and will need to adjust." [S001]
- "Many of the small schools that we invested in did not improve students’ achievement in any significant way." [S001]
- "We thought that if states raised the standards the market would respond and develop new instructional materials that aligned with those standards. That didn’t happen, so we looked for ways to encourage the market." [S005]
- "Once projects expanded to reach hundreds of thousands of students, we stopped seeing the results we hoped for." [S005]
- "In the beginning, we put a lot of resources into HIV preventatives that needed to be taken every day. For a lot of reasons, those didn’t turn out as we hoped." [S005]
