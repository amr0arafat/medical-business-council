# Bill Gates: books and written works he references

The notes for this brain contain one book that he cites by author. They also contain several reports and rating tools he points to as models. Those are listed separately and marked as such. Nothing here reproduces text from any book.

## Book

### William Rosen, a history of the steam engine (cited in the 2013 Annual Letter)

He draws on Rosen's history of the steam engine. The book's title is not given in the notes, so it is not named here. What he takes from it: precise measurement tools turned invention from a rare event into routine, step-by-step improvement. The story he highlights is a measuring device, a micrometer known as the Lord Chancellor, that let engineers see small gains and build on them (inferred from S003).

He uses the book as the origin of his own model. He says outright that the goal, measure, and feedback loop he asks every program to run is similar to the one Rosen describes (inferred from S003; the full quote sits in frameworks.md, model 1).

A related line in the same letter states his view that progress compounds once a tool exists:

- "Once these tools are invented, they are never un-invented-they just improve." [S003]

Why it matters for the council: the book is his argument that measurement is itself an innovation. A hospital that cannot measure a process cannot improve it in steps. He applies the same idea to vaccine delivery, teacher feedback, and primary care data (inferred from S003).

## Reports and rating tools he points to (not books)

These are listed for context only. They are reports or tools, not books.

### The teacher-effectiveness study (2013 letter)

He cites a report on how to measure teacher effectiveness. He describes its main finding as proof that teaching quality can be measured in a reliable way:

- "The report concluded that there were observable, repeatable, and verifiable ways of measuring teacher effectiveness." [S003]

He uses it to argue that feedback systems are worth their cost, as in the Eagle County example (inferred from S003).

### The G20 report on paying for aid (2012 letter)

He cites a G20 report on how to pay for aid. In the letter he supports its options: a modest financial transaction tax, higher tobacco taxes, and a carbon tax (inferred from S002). He is frank about its limits:

- "None of these ideas has universal agreement, and none will solve the problem alone, but they can make a big difference if even just a few countries adopt them." [S002]

### EdReports (2020 letter, Bill section)

He points to an independent rating service for school teaching materials. He describes it by analogy:

- "which acts like a Consumer Reports for instructional materials" [S005]

The lesson he draws is that third-party ratings can make a market work when buyers cannot judge quality alone (inferred from S005). For a hospital, this maps to independent ratings of suppliers or tools.

### His mother's letter (2007 Harvard speech)

In his Harvard speech he recalls a letter from his mother on the theme that much is expected (inferred from S006). He uses it to frame duty, not as a source of method.
