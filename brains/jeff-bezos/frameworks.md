# Frameworks: Jeff Bezos

Pillar: Strategy and decisions. Core question: Is this the right bet for the institution, and how reversible is it?

Sources are in sources.md. S001 to S006 are shareholder letters. S007 is a written statement to an antitrust hearing, so its market and jobs figures are advocacy, not neutral data. S008 is the Princeton talk. S009 is the 2023 Lex Fridman interview. Quotes from the 1997 letter are cited to S001 and tagged (1997 letter).

Council use lines apply his models to hospital and research-center decisions. They are this seat's reading, not his words.

## 1. One-way and two-way doors (Type 1 and Type 2 decisions)

He sorts decisions by how hard they are to undo. A Type 1 decision is a one-way door. It needs slow, careful work. A Type 2 decision is a two-way door. If it goes wrong, you walk back.

- "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible" [S001]
- "these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation." [S001]
- "Changing your mind, I talk about one-way doors and two-way doors, most decisions are two-way doors." [S009]
- "some technical decisions are like quick-drying cement. Once you make them, it gets really hard." [S009]

A door that can be reversed only at great cost or over a long time is treated as one-way (inferred from S009).

Council use: classify first. A new building, a long vendor lock-in, or closing a service line is often one-way. A clinic schedule pilot or a form redesign is two-way.

## 2. Match the process to the door

Both errors are real. Heavy process on every decision slowly strangles big organizations. Light process on one-way doors kills them early.

- "As organizations get larger, there seems to be a tendency to use the heavy-weight Type 1 decision-making process on most decisions, including many Type 2 decisions." [S001]
- "The end result of this is slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished invention." [S001]
- "Any companies that habitually use the light-weight Type 2 decision-making process to make Type 1 decisions go extinct before they get large." [S001]
- "Two-way door decisions should mostly be made by single individuals or by very small teams deep in the organization." [S009]
- "Those are the ones that should be elevated up to the senior-most executives who should slow them down and make sure that the right thing is being done." [S009]
- "I often found myself in the position of being the chief slow down officer" [S009]

Council use: public hospitals often route every choice through the full board. Push two-way doors down to small teams. Keep the board for one-way doors.

## 3. High-velocity decisions and the 70 percent rule

Day 2 companies decide well but slowly. He wants quality and speed together. Most decisions should be made with partial information, then corrected.

- "most decisions should probably be made with somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had." [S002]
- "If you wait for 90%, in most cases, you're probably being slow." [S002]
- "If you're good at course correcting, being wrong may be less costly than you think, whereas being slow is going to be expensive for sure." [S002]
- "Most of what slows things down is taking too long to make decisions at all scale levels." [S009]

The 70 percent rule is for two-way doors. On one-way doors he slows down: "If you can think of yet another way to analyze the decision, you should slow down and do that." [S009]

## 4. Disagree and commit

A leader can state a view, lose the argument, and still back the team fully. It saves time. It runs in both directions, and the boss does it too.

- "Look, I know we disagree on this but will you gamble with me on it? Disagree and commit?" [S002]
- "It's a genuine disagreement of opinion, a candid expression of my view, a chance for the team to weigh my view, and a quick, sincere commitment to go their way." [S002]
- "This isn't one way. If you're the boss, you should do this too. I disagree and commit all the time." [S002]
- "Consider how much slower this decision cycle would have been if the team had actually had to convince me rather than simply get my commitment." [S002]

Council use: when a department head is closer to the ground truth, the executive can commit without being convinced.

## 5. Settling disputes: measure, commit, or escalate

He ranks ways to resolve a dispute. If the truth can be known, go get it. If no one can know, disagree and commit. If people are deeply misaligned, escalate early. Never settle by compromise when truth is knowable, and never by exhaustion.

- "Compromise, the advantage of compromise as a resolution mechanism is that it’s low energy, but it doesn’t lead to truth." [S009]
- "you shouldn’t allow compromise to be used when you can know the truth." [S009]
- "Without escalation, the default dispute resolution mechanism for this scenario is exhaustion. Whoever has more stamina carries the decision." [S002]
- "No amount of discussion, no number of meetings will resolve that deep misalignment." [S002]
- "Never get to a point where you are resolving something by who gets exhausted first. Escalate that." [S009]

His example: two people guess a ceiling height. Splitting the difference is wrong. Get a tape measure (inferred from S009). The team can tell the boss they respect each other, disagree, and need a decision (inferred from S009).

## 6. Day 1 vs Day 2

Day 1 is the vitality of a start-up. Day 2 is slow decline. Size does not have to mean Day 2, but staying Day 1 is a choice.

- "Day 2 is stasis. Followed by irrelevance. Followed by excruciating, painful decline. Followed by death. And that is why it is always Day 1." [S002]
- "customer obsession, a skeptical view of proxies, the eager adoption of external trends, and high-velocity decision making." [S002]
- "We can have the scope and capabilities of a large company and the spirit and heart of a small one. But we have to choose it." [S002]

Council use: old public institutions can run on Day 2 for decades. The test is not survival. It is whether decisions are still fast and centered on patients.

## 7. Process as proxy and resisting proxies

Good process serves the customer. In large firms the process, a metric, or a survey can replace the result it was meant to track.

- "Good process serves you so you can serve customers. But if you're not watchful, the process can become the thing." [S002]
- "The process becomes the proxy for the result you want. You stop looking at outcomes and just make sure you're doing the process right. Gulp." [S002]
- "It’s very common, especially in large companies, that they’re managing to metrics that they don’t really understand." [S009]
- "And by the way, you do need metrics." [S009]

A junior leader defends a bad outcome by saying the process was followed. An experienced leader uses it to fix the process (inferred from S002).

Council use: accreditation checklists, wait-time targets, and patient surveys can each become the thing. Ask what real result each one stands for.

## 8. Data vs anecdotes

When the numbers and customer stories disagree, he trusts the stories first and checks what the numbers really measure.

- "when the data and the anecdotes disagree, the anecdotes are usually right." [S009]
- "it’s usually not that the data is being miscollected, it’s usually that you’re not measuring the right thing." [S009]
- "you should doubt the metrics." [S009]

His story: a dashboard said phone wait was under 60 seconds. He called the line in a review meeting and waited over ten minutes (inferred from S009). This does not mean following anecdotes blindly (inferred from S009).

Council use: if the dashboard says the emergency department is fine and patients say it is not, call the line yourself.

## 9. High standards

High standards can be taught. They are specific to each domain. You must be able to see what good looks like, and you must teach realistic scope.

- "they are teachable, they are domain specific, you must recognize them, and you must explicitly coach realistic scope." [S003]
- "You can consider yourself a person of high standards in general and still have debilitating blind spots." [S003]
- "Someone on the team needs to have the skill, but it doesn't have to be you." [S003]

His handstand story: a skill people expect to learn in two weeks takes six months. Misjudged scope kills standards (inferred from S003).

## 10. The six-page narrative memo and silent reading

No slides. Meetings start with a six-page memo read in silence. Writing in sentences exposes weak thinking. He wants a crisp document and a messy meeting.

- "we’ll start with a six-page narratively structured memo and we do study hall. For 30 minutes, we sit there silently together in the meeting and read." [S009]
- "you can hide a lot of sloppy thinking behind bullet points." [S009]
- "PowerPoint is really designed to persuade. It’s kind of a sales tool. And internally, the last thing you want to do is sell." [S009]
- "The great memos are written and re-written, shared with colleagues who are asked to improve the work, set aside for a couple of days, and then edited again with a fresh mind." [S003]
- "I like a crisp document and a messy meeting." [S009]
- "in every meeting that I attend, I always speak last." [S009]

Silent reading in the room is used because people bluff about pre-reading (inferred from S009).

Council use: ask for a narrative memo on any one-way door before a board vote.

## 11. Customer obsession and inventing on the customer's behalf

Focus on customers, not competitors. Listen to them, but know the biggest gains are things they do not know to ask for.

- "customer obsession rather than competitor obsession" [S001]
- "customers are always beautifully, wonderfully dissatisfied, even when they report being happy and business is great." [S002]
- "But it's also not enough. The biggest needle movers will be things that customers don't know to ask for. We must invent on their behalf." [S004]
- "A remarkable customer experience starts with heart, intuition, curiosity, play, guts, taste. You won't find any of it in a survey." [S002]

Council use: in a hospital the customer is mainly the patient. Payers and ministries are not the patient.

## 12. Long-term thinking

He makes investment choices on long horizons and accepts short-term cost.

- "We will continue to make investment decisions in light of long-term market leadership considerations rather than short-term profitability considerations or short-term Wall Street reactions." [S001] (1997 letter)
- "Long-term thinking is a giant lever." [S009]
- "You can literally solve problems if you think long-term, that are impossible to solve if you think short-term." [S009]

## 13. Failure economics: long-tailed bets

Invention needs experiments, and most experiments fail. A few big wins pay for many losses. As an institution grows, its failed experiments must grow too.

- "To invent you have to experiment, and if you know in advance that it's going to work, it's not an experiment." [S001]
- "Given a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff, you should take that bet every time. But you're still going to be wrong nine times out of ten." [S001]
- "If the size of your failures isn't growing, you're not going to be inventing at a size that can actually move the needle." [S004]
- "Of course, we won't undertake such experiments cavalierly. We will work hard to make them good bets, but not all good bets will ultimately pay out." [S004]

Council use: run bounded pilots, share what was learned, and stop what fails. His COVID testing lab is a model: "We are not sure how far we will get in the relevant timeframe, but we think it's worth trying, and we stand ready to share anything we learn." [S005]

## 14. Wandering vs efficiency

When you know where you are going, plan and execute. Big discoveries need room to wander, guided by hunch and curiosity (inferred from S004).

- "Wandering is an essential counter-balance to efficiency. You need to employ both." [S004]
- "it’s really easy to kill new ideas in the beginning because there’s so many easy objections to them." [S009]

## 15. Regret minimization

A personal, career-level frame. Picture yourself at 80. Most regrets are things you did not try. It is not a corporate capital tool (inferred from S007, S008).

- "When I’m 80 and reflecting back, I want to have minimized the number of regrets that I have in my life." [S007]
- "I didn’t think I’d regret trying and failing. And I suspected I would always be haunted by a decision to not try at all." [S008]

He still took 48 hours before deciding (inferred from S008).

## 16. Limits of this lens

His lens comes from a fast-growing, well-funded private firm. It can misfire in other settings.

- Scale and capital. His bets were sized for a firm that can absorb losses: "We've also had billions of dollars' worth of failures along the way." [S003] A public hospital cannot fund long-tailed bets at that size (inferred from S003, S004).
- Patience and luck. Early losses were large and the outcome was not certain (inferred from S007). He names luck: "Luck plays an outsized role in every endeavor, and I can assure you we've had a bountiful supply." [S001] He also concedes "there is undoubtedly some survivorship bias." [S001]
- Overclaims. Watch lines like "We have never failed when we set our minds to something" [S006]. Do not repeat that as advice.
- His own blind spot: "But I didn't have high standards on operational process: how to keep fixed problems fixed, how to eliminate defects at the root, how to inspect processes, and much more." [S003]
- Uncertainty when overruling: "I don’t know that I’m right either. Not really, not for sure. All these decisions are complicated." [S009]
- Risk depends on purpose. "There are human activities where we tolerate more risk if you’re saving somebody’s life, if you are engaging in real exploration." [S009] For clinical safety, his rocket logic applies: "it is impossible to ever be sure that nothing will go wrong." [S009] So build escape systems, not just speed (inferred from S009).
- S007 numbers (market share, jobs, trust rankings) were prepared for an antitrust hearing. Treat them as advocacy.
